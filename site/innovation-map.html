Routes to innovation

How research at the Centre for Digital Neurotechnology could reach industry and patients, in four tracks: through collaborators' networks, with established medtech and pharma, with Nordic neurotech companies, and through spin-outs.

11 November 2026. Built from public data (publications, projects, patents, funders' and companies' pages). Ratings and interpretations are a first proposal for discussion.

Summary

Where the centre stands

Strong science and clinical links, few direct industry links: of 400 publications since 2016, 62 have a hospital co-author and 6 a company co-author. One US patent granted (2023), and a record of Simula companies in scientific computing (Kalkulo, Expert Analytics) and cardiac modelling (Organos, Insilicomed).

Four tracks

Collaborators already publish with 73 companies, and partner centres such as ProCardio bring GE HealthCare and Medtronic close. Large companies need a defined problem and credible models. Of 119 Nordic neurotech companies mapped, 29 are active and a high fit. Spin-outs need protectable results and founders.

Leading opportunities

Heart-to-brain drug testing: patents, Organos, and regulators moving away from animal testing. Clearance biomarkers: strong clinical need and no validated biomarker yet. Digital-twin engineering: mature software and Simula's record of consultancies.

What it takes

People more than instruments: a contact for each relationship, research software engineers, and founders, often postdocs or students. Funding within reach: ERC Proof of Concept, the EU virtual human twin call (June 2027), industrial PhDs and SkatteFUNN.

Part 2

The Norwegian landscape

Where neurotechnology succeeds, and what Norway has and lacks, from public reports and news (November 2026).

Where neurotechnology succeeds, and why

  • United States. Most of the capital and the large rounds (Neuralink, Synchron, Precision), the first regulatory approvals (Precision Neuroscience, Medtronic's adaptive deep brain stimulation, 2025), and large device companies that buy start-ups (Boston Scientific and Nalu).
  • Switzerland. The Wyss Center, a donor-funded translational centre (CHF 120 million commitment), on a shared campus with EPFL, the University of Geneva and university hospitals; ONWARD Medical came out of this setting.
  • United Kingdom. ARIA, a government agency funding a £69 million neurotechnology programme with 19 teams.
  • Denmark. Foundation capital: the Novo Nordisk Foundation's DKK 5.5 billion to the BioInnovation Institute, and Medicon Valley, a cross-border life-science cluster with Sweden.
  • Finland. A MEG and EEG legacy from Helsinki University of Technology to today's MEGIN.

Common factors: translational institutes that take technical risk, specialist and foundation capital, large companies that buy start-ups, a fast regulatory route to a first market, and university hospitals willing to run first-in-human studies.

What Norway has

  • World-class systems neuroscience (Kavli Institute, Nobel Prize 2014).
  • Population cohorts and registries linked by personal number (HUNT, registry linkage), and a national service for sensitive health data from 2026.
  • One public hospital system with joint procurement.
  • Medtech built on imaging and signals: GE Vingmed Ultrasound, NordicNeuroLab, Holberg EEG.
  • A notified body for medical devices in Norway (DNV), and a health industry with NOK 76 billion turnover.
  • New infrastructure: the Life Science Building and Oslo Science City; technology transfer through Inven2 and others.

What Norway lacks

  • Venture capital: about 4 basis points of GDP, the lowest among the Nordic countries; public seed and venture investment about €88 million a year, against €278 million in Denmark.
  • Few specialist health investors (Nordic Life Science, Aleap Ventures); access to capital is "extremely challenging" for new companies (Inven2).
  • A small home market, slow commercialisation and difficult access to health data, as named in the 2019 health industry white paper.
  • Dependence on imported medical equipment, and no Norwegian neurotechnology deals or exits found.
  • Researchers stay in academia: about half of PhD graduates work in universities and institutes.
  • No foundation or institute comparable to the Novo Nordisk Foundation or the Wyss Center.

How to go about it

Large companies rarely fund open-ended research. They pay for answers to defined problems, on their own timelines, and decide in their research headquarters rather than in their Nordic sales offices. The way in is nearly always a person: a clinician who already works with the company, a collaborator, or a partner centre. Five things make the difference:

  • A named problem and a named contact. One page per company: the problem the centre can solve, the evidence, what is delivered (a validated model, a software module, an in-silico study), and on what terms.
  • Documented credibility of models. Regulators accept computational evidence when its credibility is documented: the US Food and Drug Administration's guidance on computational modelling in medical device submissions builds on the ASME V&V 40 standard. A model documented to that standard (verification, validation, uncertainty quantification) is something companies can use, and something few academic groups offer.
  • Start small, and paid. A feasibility study or contract research, an industrial PhD, a master's project, or the company as partner in a Research Council innovation project or an EU application (IHI, the virtual human twin call).
  • Agreements settled early. Who owns what (existing know-how and new results), publication rights, and how open-source software is handled, with principles agreed with Simula's innovation and legal support before the first contract.
  • Patience. These relationships take years and outlast single projects. ProCardio's industry consortium builds on an earlier SFI, the Center for Cardiological Innovation.

What it takes

  • A protectable result and a paying customer. Inventions disclosed to Simula Innovation before publication.
  • Founders. Usually a postdoc or researcher, with a business co-founder (see part 4).
  • Regulation and reimbursement. Software that informs diagnosis or treatment is a medical device under EU MDR; hospital sales follow clearance and reimbursement (icometrix, HeartFlow). Quality systems and clinical evidence are needed from the start, unless the first customers are drug trials and research.
  • A validation partner. A pharma company, CRO or hospital that co-validates the product on its own data.
  • Open source. How a company relates to the centre's open-source software: services and support on open code, or a separate closed product.
  • Early funding. ERC Proof of Concept, Research Council qualification and verification, Simula Innovation, Simula Garage and the early-phase innovation platform; later EIC Transition and Accelerator.

1 · Heart-to-brain drug testing

In-silico translation of drug effects from stem-cell-derived tissue to adult human heart and brain cells

1 · Network2 · Large companies3 · Neurotech companies4 · Spin-outs
Maturity highPull highPartners high
Idea
The cardiac methods behind the centre's patents extended to neurons and glia, as an analysis layer on stem-cell and organoid assays for drug safety and efficacy.
Why now
The US FDA plans to phase out animal-testing requirements in favour of human-based methods, including computer models and organoids.
Builds on
Four patent families (Tveito, Jæger), one granted in the US in 2023 and held by Organos; IdentiPhy (2020–2022) and SIMBER (2021–2026) with UC Berkeley; EMIx, Scales and DigiCells.
By track
1: Organos, the Hybrid Technology Hub, MaxWell Biosystems. 2: pharma through Healy's network. 3: Nordic neural-assay labs (BrainZell, Oscillution, Zenit Science, Cellectricon). 4: licensing through Simula Innovation or Organos.
Existing companies
Oxford's Virtual Assay (in-silico cardiac drug trials, developed with Janssen); Certara and InSilicoTrials (in-silico platforms); TARA Biosystems (engineered heart tissue, acquired by Valo Health in 2022). None found that extends to neural cells.
Possible first step
A review of which patents remain active and whether they cover neural cells.

2 · Clearance biomarkers

MRI-based measures of brain-fluid flow and clearance, for drug trials first and the clinic later

1 · Network2 · Large companies3 · Neurotech companies4 · Spin-outs
Maturity mediumPull highPartners medium
Idea
Quantitative measures of cerebrospinal-fluid flow and solute clearance from MRI, calibrated with the centre's physics-based models and tracer-MRI data: first as endpoints in drug trials, later as decision support for dementia and normal-pressure hydrocephalus.
Why now
Anti-amyloid antibodies require MRI monitoring for brain swelling and bleeding (ARIA); drugs to enhance clearance are in development; no validated clearance biomarker exists yet.
Builds on
aCleanBrain, the K. G. Jebsen Centre, Waterscape and Waterscales, AlzheimersPhysics; imaging at Oslo University Hospital. No patents yet.
By track
1: the K. G. Jebsen Centre and Oslo University Hospital. 2: MRI vendors (Siemens Healthineers, Philips) and pharma with brain-targeting drugs (Roche). 3: Nordic companies in brain fluids, pressure and perfusion (Likvor, Brainlink, Nisonic, CSF-Dynamics, Cercare). 4: a possible spin-out.
Existing companies
icometrix (FDA-cleared brain MRI analysis, including ARIA); IXICO (imaging analysis for neurology trials); Applied Cognition (wearable glymphatic monitoring, validated against contrast MRI); Cercare (model-based perfusion maps).
Possible first step
A shared clinical question and data set with Oslo University Hospital, and a check of what can be protected before publication; ERC Proof of Concept as early funding.
